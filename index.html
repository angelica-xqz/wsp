II — Stratigraphy

What Lies Beneath

A descent through the layers of Washington Square Park, from the surface most photographed to the ground we stand on but rarely see.

Layer 01 · Surface

The Familiar Park

Present

The arch, the fountain, the pianist, the chess tables, the dogs, the graduation crowds, the tourists, the NYU students between classes. A 9.75-acre stage so densely scripted that to walk through it is to confirm what is already known.

The photographs converge on the same five vantages. The captions write themselves. The park is, in the most precise sense, over-seen: looked at constantly, registered superficially, recognized only at its surface.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Field observation, 2026
Layer 02 · Root & Soil — 0.5 to 3 m

The Compacted Ground

Continuously formed

Beneath the pavers and lawns: a substrate compacted by more than a century of foot traffic, redesigns, water and gas mains, and the unstable settlement caused by a buried stream. Roots negotiate around utility lines. Soil oxygen is limited. Some of the park's English elms, including the so-called Hangman's Elm in the northwest corner, have stood for over three centuries; others die quietly and are replaced.

This is the slow violence Nixon describes — environmental change too gradual to register as an event, distributed across decades and species. A tree that takes thirty years to die does not produce news.

Source · NYC Parks tree map · Geismar, Phase 2 Archaeological Field Report, 2012
Layer 03 · Minetta Brook — 3 to 6 m

Devil's Water, Buried

c. 1808 — present

One of the largest natural watercourses in pre-colonial Manhattan, Minetta Brook ran from west of the present arch southward through the park. The Lenape called it Manette — devil's water. The Dutch called it Mintje Kill.

By 1808 the city was filling and diverting the stream to stabilize the new potter's field. By 1828 it had been fully culverted. It still flows today, beneath the pavement, surfacing occasionally in basements along Minetta Street, which was platted to follow its original curvature. The fountain at the park's center sits roughly above the buried channel.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Viele Sanitary & Topographical Map, 1865 · Village Preservation
Layer 04 · Potter's Field — 1.5 to 3.5 m

Twenty Thousand Buried

1797 — 1825

In April 1797, in the wake of repeated yellow fever epidemics, the city's Common Council acquired this land for use as a public burial ground. Originally designated for roughly 5,000 graves, the site received four times that capacity over the next thirty years. An estimated 20,000 people remain buried here: the indigent, the unidentified, victims of yellow fever, enslaved and formerly enslaved Africans, the city's poor.

Construction during the park's 2008–2017 redesign repeatedly disturbed intact burials. In 2009 a tombstone was unearthed bearing the name James Jackson, an Irish immigrant who died in 1799 at age 28. In 2015, water main work uncovered two 19th-century burial vaults containing roughly thirty bodies. The bones were reinterred in 2021 under a paver near the Sullivan Street entrance. The official park signage does not mention any of this.

Source · NYC Parks Department, Washington Square Park Highlights · Geismar, Archaeological Field Report, 2009 · Washington Square Park Blog, 2022
Layer 05 · Land of the Blacks — 3 to 5 m

A Black Farmstead, Erased

1645 — 1716

In 1645, after negotiating their freedom from the Dutch West India Company, formerly enslaved Africans received land grants on what is now Washington Square Park. Anthony Portuguese received the first grant; Manuel Trumpeter followed. They farmed much of the southern park.

The area became known as the Land of the Blacks. When the English took over the colony in 1664, they began withdrawing the rights of free Black landholders. By 1716, all of these properties had been claimed by white landowners. The official park history records this; the park itself does not.

Source · NYC Parks Department, Washington Square Park Highlights
Layer 06 · Sapokanikan — Pre-1624

The Tobacco Land

Time immemorial — c. 1700

Before the park, before the burial ground, before the farmstead: a marshland fed by Minetta Brook, gathering place of the Lenape, who called the broader region Sapokanikan — Land of Tobacco Growth. The trail leading from the village to inland planting fields later became the northern boundary of what is now the park.

The Lenape did not conceive of land as something that could be sold. By the early 1700s, the few hundred who had survived European settlement and disease were forced from Manhattan. The land changed hands. The marsh was filled. The brook was buried. The park was named after a man who never walked here. The Lenape, despite the displacement, are not gone. Lenapehoking persists.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Village Preservation, 2021 · Washington Square Park Conservancy

Six layers, one ground. The park you walk through is the thinnest of them all.

IV — Slow Time

The Pace of Change

Slow violence, Rob Nixon writes, is a violence "incremental and accretive, its calamitous repercussions playing out across a range of temporal scales." A park does not change in a day.

Then
The Arch, newly completed in marble
c. 1900 · Detroit Publishing Co. / Library of Congress
Now
The same arch, 126 years later
2026 · Spring, looking north
Then
Minetta Brook, surfacing across Greenwich Village
1865 · Egbert L. Viele, Sanitary & Topographical Map
Now

A century apart, the arch is the same building. The crowd is not. In Detroit Publishing Co.'s 1900 photograph, the plaza is nearly empty; in 2026, the same vantage produces a different image entirely. Slow violence rarely arrives as an event, but its accretions sit in the difference between two photographs.

V — Field Notes

What I Saw

Photographs and short observations from the park, organized around what tends to fall outside the dominant frame.

No. 01 · The Photographable Park

Cherry Blossoms, the Arch, the Crowd

I am including this photograph the way Sontag includes hers — as evidence of my own visual habits. Spring in Washington Square Park produces this image by the thousand: low angle, blossoms in the foreground, the arch as monument, a soft sky.

It is not a bad photograph. It is the photograph the park asks for. Its repetition is the point. To begin a project like this is also to admit the photographs you have already taken.

No. 02 · The Park's Other Inhabitants

A Pigeon in the Undergrowth

Behind a low fence, in a patch of fern and turned earth, a pigeon. The fence is meant to keep people out of the planting bed; it does not stop the bird. Almost no photographs of Washington Square Park show its non-human residents at this scale — the squirrels, the rats, the resident hawks, the pigeons that out-populate the visitors on most days.

The park is also their park. The dominant image repertoire does not picture this.

No. 03 · Canopy & Infrastructure

Lane Narrows

Late afternoon, the eastern entrance. A construction sign reads Lane Narrows. Beyond it: tree canopy filtering the light, the fountain barely visible, a lamppost, a crowd reading as a smudge of figures. Behind everything, the construction barrier — green plywood — interrupts the romantic frame.

The park is constantly under maintenance. The maintenance is part of the park. We tend to crop it out.

No. 04 · The Skyline View

The Most Photographed Vantage

From a high floor of an NYU building, the canonical aerial: arch at center, fountain below, the surrounding canopy in May green, the Empire State Building visible to the north, Hudson Yards in the distance, the park as composed scene.

This is the image that sells the apartment, the brochure, the postcard. It is also a kind of looking that flattens the ground beneath into background. Beneath this view: the buried brook, the unmarked graves, the soil that does not register on any of these surfaces.

VI — About & Sources

Notes on Method

A project built from public archives and one student's looking. What you found is what was used.

The Project

Beneath the Arch is a research and media project about Washington Square Park as a site of recognitional and procedural environmental injustice — about who and what the park is seen to contain, who participates in deciding what it becomes, and which harms register as harms at all.

The project was made by Angelica Hsu in spring 2026 for MCC-UE 1027 (Media & Environment) at NYU Steinhardt, taught by Prof. Kendra Kintzi. Coding assistance and proofreading were aided by AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT) per course policy; all writing, source selection, argumentation, and design decisions are the author's own.

A Note on What This Is Not

The project relies on existing archives, public datasets, and the author's own photographic practice — not on original ecological fieldwork or interview-based research. A fuller version would include observational interviews with non-NYU regular users of the park (chess players, Parks Department workers, longtime neighbors) and tree-by-tree health observations. Within a one-semester scope, I have chosen to do fewer things more rigorously, and to be transparent about what the project is not.
