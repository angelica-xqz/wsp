II · Stratigraphy

What Lies Beneath

Scroll down through what's underneath the park, the layers you don't see when you're crossing it on the way to class.

Layer 01 · Surface

The Familiar Park

Present

The arch, the fountain, the pianist, the chess tables, the dogs, the graduation crowds, the tourists, the NYU students between classes. A 9.75-acre stage so densely scripted that to walk through it is to confirm what is already known.

The photographs converge on the same five vantages. The captions write themselves. The park is, in the most precise sense, over-seen: looked at constantly, registered superficially, recognized only at its surface.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Field observation, 2026
Layer 02 · Root & Soil, 0.5 to 3 m

The Compacted Ground

Continuously formed

Beneath the pavers and lawns: a substrate compacted by more than a century of foot traffic, redesigns, water and gas mains, and the unstable settlement caused by a buried stream. Roots negotiate around utility lines. Soil oxygen is limited. Some of the park's English elms, including the so-called Hangman's Elm in the northwest corner, have stood for over three centuries; others die quietly and are replaced.

This is the slow violence Nixon describes, environmental change too gradual to register as an event, distributed across decades and species. A tree that takes thirty years to die does not produce news.

Source · NYC Parks tree map · Geismar, Phase 2 Archaeological Field Report, 2012
Layer 03 · Minetta Brook, 3 to 6 m

Devil's Water, Buried

c. 1808, present

One of the largest natural watercourses in pre-colonial Manhattan, Minetta Brook ran from west of the present arch southward through the park. The Lenape called it Manette, devil's water. The Dutch called it Mintje Kill.

By 1808 the city was filling and diverting the stream to stabilize the new potter's field. By 1828 it had been fully culverted. It still flows today, beneath the pavement, surfacing occasionally in basements along Minetta Street, which was platted to follow its original curvature. The fountain at the park's center sits roughly above the buried channel.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Viele Sanitary & Topographical Map, 1865 · Village Preservation
Layer 04 · Potter's Field, 1.5 to 3.5 m

Twenty Thousand Buried

1797–1825

In April 1797, in the wake of repeated yellow fever epidemics, the city's Common Council acquired this land for use as a public burial ground. Originally designated for roughly 5,000 graves, the site received four times that capacity over the next thirty years. An estimated 20,000 people remain buried here: the indigent, the unidentified, victims of yellow fever, enslaved and formerly enslaved Africans, the city's poor.

Construction during the park's 2008–2017 redesign repeatedly disturbed intact burials. In 2009 a tombstone was unearthed bearing the name James Jackson, an Irish immigrant who died in 1799 at age 28. In 2015, water main work uncovered two 19th-century burial vaults containing roughly thirty bodies. The bones were reinterred in 2021 under a paver near the Sullivan Street entrance. The official park signage does not mention any of this.

Source · NYC Parks Department, Washington Square Park Highlights · Geismar, Archaeological Field Report, 2009 · Washington Square Park Blog, 2022
Layer 05 · Land of the Blacks, 3 to 5 m

A Black Farmstead, Erased

1645–1716

In 1645, after negotiating their freedom from the Dutch West India Company, formerly enslaved Africans received land grants on what is now Washington Square Park. Anthony Portuguese received the first grant; Manuel Trumpeter followed. They farmed much of the southern park.

The area became known as the Land of the Blacks. When the English took over the colony in 1664, they began withdrawing the rights of free Black landholders. By 1716, all of these properties had been claimed by white landowners. The official park history records this; the park itself does not.

Source · NYC Parks Department, Washington Square Park Highlights
Layer 06 · Sapokanikan, Pre-1624

The Tobacco Land

Time immemorial, c. 1700

Before the park, before the burial ground, before the farmstead: a marshland fed by Minetta Brook, gathering place of the Lenape, who called the broader region Sapokanikan, Land of Tobacco Growth. The trail leading from the village to inland planting fields later became the northern boundary of what is now the park.

The Lenape did not conceive of land as something that could be sold. By the early 1700s, the few hundred who had survived European settlement and disease were forced from Manhattan. The land changed hands. The marsh was filled. The brook was buried. The park was named after a man who never walked here. The Lenape, despite the displacement, are not gone. Lenapehoking persists.

Source · NYC Parks Department · Village Preservation, 2021 · Washington Square Park Conservancy

Six layers under one ground. The park you walk through is the thinnest of them.

The Pace of Change

V · Field Notes

What I Saw

My own photos, with short notes underneath. Some of these are pictures I caught myself almost not taking.

No. 01 · The Photographable Park

Cherry Blossoms, the Arch, the Crowd

I'm including this one the way Sontag includes hers, as evidence of my own visual habits. Every spring, the park makes this exact photograph: low angle, blossoms in the foreground, the arch as monument, the sky behind.

It's not a bad photograph. It's the one the park asks for. The repetition is the point. Part of starting a project like this is admitting the photos you've already taken.

No. 02 · The Park's Other Inhabitants

A Pigeon in the Undergrowth

Behind a low fence, in a patch of fern and turned earth: a pigeon. The fence keeps people out of the planting bed. It doesn't stop the bird. Almost nobody photographs the park's non-human residents at this scale, the squirrels, the rats, the hawks, the pigeons that outnumber the visitors most days.

The park is also theirs. The standard image repertoire just doesn't include them.

No. 03 · Canopy & Infrastructure

Lane Narrows

Late afternoon at the eastern entrance. A construction sign says Lane Narrows. Behind it: canopy filtering the light, the fountain barely there, a lamppost, a crowd reading mostly as a smudge. Further back, the green plywood of the construction barrier breaks up the otherwise pretty frame.

The park is always under some kind of maintenance. The maintenance is part of the park. We just usually crop it out.

No. 04 · The Skyline View

The Most Photographed Vantage

From a high floor of an NYU building: the textbook aerial. Arch at center, fountain below, canopy in May green, Empire State Building to the north, Hudson Yards in the distance, the whole park reading like a composed scene.

This is the image that sells the apartment, the brochure, the postcard. It's also a kind of looking that turns everything underneath into background. Underneath: the buried brook, the unmarked graves, soil that doesn't show up on any of these surfaces.

VI · About & Sources

Notes on Method

Where this project comes from, what it draws on, and what it admits it isn't.

The Project

Beneath the Arch is a research and media project about Washington Square Park as a site of recognitional and procedural environmental injustice, about who and what the park is seen to contain, who gets to decide what it becomes, and which harms ever register as harms at all.

Made by Angelica Hsu in spring 2026 for MCC-UE 1027 (Media & Environment) at NYU Steinhardt, with Prof. Kendra Kintzi. AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT) helped with coding and proofreading per course policy; the writing, source selection, argument, and design choices are mine.

Relevance: Five Concepts from Class

The project engages five readings from the syllabus and uses each one to do specific work on the park.

Lakoff on framing: WSP gets named in dominant media as a campus quad, a tourist photograph, a Village amenity. Almost never as a still-active burial ground or a contested commons. The Counter-Atlas section doesn't argue against the existing frames so much as install alternative ones next to them.

Cronon on wilderness: a strange fit for a Manhattan park, until you notice that "wilderness" trains us to see only distant landscapes as nature, and to miss the nature embedded in everyday places. WSP, a soil ecosystem with roots, water, and decomposing bodies, is exactly the kind of place the wilderness frame disqualifies.

Nixon on slow violence: the harms in WSP are not events. Trees die over decades from soil compaction. Public-space norms erode through small redesigns and donor agreements. Buried histories disappear through generations of paving. Slow Time and Stratigraphy make that gradual harm legible.

Liboiron on rhetorical functions of data: data is never neutral. The Counter-Atlas extends Liboiron's argument from data to cartography: WSP has an official cartographic infrastructure (Parks Dept map, Conservancy amenities map) that represents what is administratively legible and not what matters to most users or to the park's history.

Sontag on representational politics: the park is one of the most photographed in the world, but the photographs converge on a narrow repertoire. Whole categories of WSP life, the labor, the unhoused, the chess players when nobody's watching, are systematically under-photographed. Field Notes and the participatory gallery both respond directly to this.

Conceptual Framing: Four Sources from Beyond the Syllabus

David Schlosberg distinguishes among distributive, procedural, and recognitional justice. Most environmental justice scholarship focuses on distribution; Schlosberg argues that justice also requires recognition (whose presences and histories are acknowledged) and procedure (who participates in decisions). My project makes a recognitional and procedural argument about WSP, not a distributional one, and Schlosberg gives me the vocabulary to do that without overclaiming.

Setha Low on the politics of public space: parks aren't neutral amenities but contested arenas in which belonging, policing, and participation are continuously negotiated. Her writing on park conservancies, donor influence, and design as a tool of exclusion is directly applicable to WSP, private conservancy since 2013, donor-influenced redesign in the 2000s, long history of enforcement against users who don't fit the park's desired image.

Joan Geismar's archaeological field reports form the empirical foundation of the buried-history layer. Geismar served as consulting archaeologist during the 2005–2013 redesign and documented intact 18th- and 19th-century burials, the path of Minetta Brook, the bounds of the former Potter's Field, and the recovery of James Jackson's headstone. Her reports give the buried-history argument archaeological backing rather than purely interpretive grounding.

Wolch, Byrne & Newell on "just green enough" urban greening show that investments in parks can drive gentrification and displace the same communities they ostensibly serve. WSP hasn't been recently "greened" in the High Line sense, but the broader claim, that beautification and inequity can move together, informs the project's skepticism toward the assumption that improving a park is the same thing as serving the public.

Empirical Sources

The project draws on five empirical bodies, all secondary or archival, chosen specifically to support the recognitional and procedural argument:

1. Joan Geismar's archaeological field reports. They source the Counter-Atlas historical pins and the Stratigraphy section, documenting the former Potter's Field, the culverted path of Minetta Brook, and specific intact burials.

2. NYC Open Data & the NYC Parks Department's public materials. Permit data, the official park map, and Conservancy programming. Together, they make up the institutional frame the Counter-Atlas works against.

3. Media coverage as primary texts. The WSP Conservancy's public communications, NYC Parks's WSP page, plus selected coverage from outlets representing distinct framing positions: The New York Times (real estate and lifestyle), Hellgate and Gothamist (critical local), the Washington Square Park Blog (independent grassroots), and NYU institutional channels.

4. NYC Municipal Archives & NYPL Digital Collections. 19th-century maps showing the burial ground and Minetta Brook, plus historical photographs that anchor Slow Time's archival comparisons.

5. My own photography, produced over the second half of the project window. A primary source for Field Notes and for the participatory gallery.

A Note on What This Is Not

The project leans on existing archives, public datasets, and my own photographic practice, not on original ecological fieldwork or interview-based research. A fuller version would include observational interviews with non-NYU regular users of the park (chess players, Parks Department workers, longtime neighbors) and tree-by-tree health observations. Within a one-semester scope, I chose to do fewer things more rigorously, and to be transparent about what the project is not.

If I keep developing this, I'd like to take it past the Counter-Atlas into something more like a digital museum for Washington Square Park, a kind of standing archive future NYU students could add to.
